# Asset table

Throwaway spike of the assets research. It runs the [reference workload](../reference-workload/README.md) with its repulsion parameters read through asset handles: every entity names an asset by a 128-bit identifier, and a world's asset table says which assets are wanted, by whom, and at which content hash each is available. It measures, instead of assuming, what the rules proposed for that table give and cost: what a handle costs on a hot pass; whether wants owned by entities, by the world and by other assets make availability a function of the inputs; whether a loading gate evaluated on the pending input record stops a running world and applies that record in one piece; whether a revision of the content reaches a world whole; whether the table belongs under the state hash; what a saved world asks when another process reads it, as a new run and to continue its history; and whether sixteen worlds of one process hold one copy of a canonical part.

The [late asset arrival spike](../workload-asset-arrival/README.md) measured the fence itself on one asset; this spike measures what is built on it.

## Layout

- `src/table.rs`: the asset identity core. A canonical part is bytes with a content hash (FNV-1a 64): the parameters, the identifiers of the assets it depends on, an opaque payload. The resident store holds the parts of the process by content hash, immutable, shared by its worlds. The asset table holds, per asset that has a want, its wants in the order of their owners (the world's own entry, an entity, an instance record, or the asset whose part lists this one) with their policy (required or optional), and its availability: unavailable, available at a hash, failed with a reason code. Applying the input record of a tick does, in this order: the falls the state owes (an asset no want names is removed), the commands (want, release, instantiate), then the batch of arrivals: a want of every dependency the batch's parts list, to any depth, owned by the parent and under its policy, then the arrivals in identifier order, one for an asset no want names changing nothing. An arrival that is no answer to a restore emits an event (arrived, hash changed, failed); the spike's one consumer, an instance record, is instantiated on the first, again on the second where its reload setting is on, and failed on the third. The gate is evaluated on a copy of the table to which the pending record is applied without the commands that add a want. The file also holds the canonical bytes of the table and of the input log, and the view the passes read: an open-addressed index from the identifier to the parameters, rebuilt when the table changes.
- `src/world.rs`: a trimmed copy of the [runtime skeleton](../runtime-skeleton/README.md) spike's core with the table as world state. The repulsion pass resolves the identifier each alive slot names once per chunk and distinct identifier, in a cache of the worker emptied by a change of chunk, then reads the cached parameters; the copied kernel takes the parameters as arguments, the same operations in the same order as the specification's. Two other forms exist for the cost: one runtime value for every slot and no asset (the parameterized world of the late asset arrival spike), and a 64-bit generational handle into a dense table whose entry holds the identifier. The input fixed point of a tick assembles the pending record (the scripted records due, the host's commands, the arrivals drained from the queue, a later arrival replacing an earlier one for the same asset), evaluates the gate on it, and applies it in one piece or leaves everything pending; inside a step it runs in the structural node, which runs alone, and the record of the tick after a step's last one is left to the next step. The structural node also ends the wants of the entities the workload despawns. A step answers with the ticks that ran and, at a closed gate, what is unanswered, at once or after a deadline. The state hash of a tick is the workload's tick hash and the hash of the table's canonical bytes hashed together. A world is saved as its components and its table, and read back as a new run (gated until every asset its table holds as available is answered by an arrival marked as an answer to a restore) or to continue its history (every such asset confirmed at the same hash before the first tick, outside the input record, or the read refused).
- `src/loader.rs`: the content and the loading capability, outside the domain. The catalog draws its identifiers once from a fixed stream: eight leaves the entities name (slot `i` names leaf `i` modulo 8), two groups that list leaves, two roots that list a group (a closure of three levels), a root whose artifact is cut short, a root nothing answers, an asset no entity names, a large asset, and a pool of 1 024 assets with distinct contents and the specification's parameters. The loader is a thread: it receives the wanted assets a world extracts as unanswered, follows their closure in its manifest, sleeps a real delay, makes every part resident (the hash looked up first, the artifact read on a miss) and posts the closure's arrivals as one batch; it answers a restore with marked arrivals; and it lands the payloads of a revision one by one, in a random order over a spread, posting their batch once all are resident.
- `src/oracle.rs`: the sequential oracle. It knows nothing of wants, batches or gates: a scenario hands it, written by hand from the rules under test, which leaf reads which parameters from which tick.
- `src/main.rs`: the scenarios, the report, the counting allocator, and the reader of a save that runs as a process of its own.

## Hypotheses

Stated before the run. Reference workload, version 1, default size (10 000 entities, 1 000 ticks), wander system off, one pinned Rust toolchain, default target features, worker counts 1, 4 and 8.

1. A handle's cost. With 1, 16 and 1 024 distinct assets named by the entities of the repulsion pass, the world runs within 5 percent of the world that reads one runtime value and has no asset, at 1 and 4 workers. A cost above 5 percent at 1 024 assets refutes it, and would speak for a generational handle into the table beside the identifier, whose cost is recorded for that comparison.
2. Wants. An asset wanted by two entities stays available when one is despawned and falls to unavailable at the input fixed point of the tick after the second is, with no element in the input record; the dependencies of an arriving part are wanted by the core at that fixed point and answered by the same batch; an arrival for an asset no want names changes nothing; the hashes equal the sequential oracle's at every tick, at every worker count, on every cell. One differing hash refutes it.
3. The gate during a run. A required want applied at a tick stops the world at the next tick boundary until its closure has been answered, whatever the loader's delay; a required asset that fails opens the gate as a transition, emits its event, and the step's result names it. While gated the world applies nothing: the gate is evaluated on the pending record, a pending command that releases the required want opens it, and the tick that follows applies the whole record once, a closure of three levels being answered by one batch. A tick that ran with a required asset unanswered, or a record applied in more than one piece, refutes it.
4. A revision as one batch. Eight transitions of one revision, whose payloads land in a random order over 200 milliseconds, are applied at one fixed point in identifier order; the recorded log replays the run; no tick's hash equals that of a world that applied a part of the batch; two revisions posted before one drain reach the world as one merged batch, each asset at its later hash. A partial application, or an asset left at the earlier hash, refutes it.
5. The table under the hash. Two worlds equal in every component and different in one asset's hash have different state hashes, and the table's canonical bytes are identical across cells. Equal state hashes, or one differing byte across cells, refute it.
6. A save read by another process. A world saved at a tick and read by a new process as a new run is gated until answered; with answers to the same hashes its hashes equal the uninterrupted run's from that tick; with one marked transition to another hash it equals the oracle that changes that asset at that tick, no consumer applying a reload and no instance being instantiated again; with one failure it reports, fails the instance and steps. The same save read to continue its history is refused, naming the hash, where one part is at another hash or cannot be had; where every part is confirmed it replays with the recorded input record, unchanged. A first tick that runs before every answer, an event or a new instantiation on an answer, a confirmation found in the record of a continued history, or a continued history that runs under another hash refutes it.
7. One resident copy. Sixteen worlds of one process that want one asset hold one copy of its canonical part, counted by the allocation counters, each with its own availability. A second copy refutes it.

## Method

Every scenario compares the workload's tick hashes with the sequential reference or with the oracle of its own schedule, and the state hashes across worker counts.

- A handle's cost: at 1 and 4 workers, five worlds (one value and no asset; identifiers with 1, 16 and 1 024 distinct assets; generational indices with 1 024), each required set answered by a scripted batch at the first tick, each world after a warm-up, 1 000 ticks, five alternating rounds. Reported per world: the best and the median rate, their ratios to the world with no asset, the identifiers resolved per tick, and the allocations counted during the timed step.
- Wants: scripted, with no loader, so that one run hash is expected on every cell. Root A is required by the composition and answered at the first tick. An arrival for the asset no entity names is scripted at a twentieth of the run. Two entities, chosen as the first slots the workload's own structural changes despawn between three and five tenths and between six and eight tenths of the run, want leaf 4; the world wants root B, whose closure is answered by one batch, then releases it; leaf 4 is wanted again by the world near the end and answered at another hash. The host stops at each tick of interest and reads the table. The recorded log is read back from its bytes and replayed at another worker count.
- The gate: a live loader, with a delay drawn from the clock between 20 and 150 ms, 0 ms and 100 ms, at every worker count. The composition requires root A and leaf 4. A scripted command makes an instance from root B, required, at a fifth of the run; another makes an instance from the corrupt root at two fifths; a third wants, as required, the root nothing answers at three fifths. There the host steps without waiting, then with a deadline of 30 ms, then posts two commands (a release of leaf 4 and the release of the unanswered root) and steps to the end.
- A revision: a live loader; every leaf required; at a tenth of the run the host hands the loader a revision of the eight leaves and steps on in batches of ten ticks; at eight tenths it posts two revisions on the queue before the next step (every leaf, then the leaves 0 to 3 again). The seven worlds that would have applied the first one to seven payloads to land, and no other, are computed by the oracle up to the tick of the batch. The log is replayed at every worker count.
- The table under the hash: two small worlds that want the asset no entity names, answered at two different hashes.
- A save: a scripted world with every leaf required, an instance made from leaf 4 with its reload setting on, leaf 4 reloaded at three tenths of the run, the save taken at half, leaf 6 reloaded at seven tenths. The save, the later records, the expected hashes and the parts (once whole, once without the part of leaf 4) are written to a scratch directory, and six readers run one after the other, each as a new process of the spike's own binary: three new runs (a content set at the saved hashes, one with leaf 4 at another hash, one with leaf 4's artifact cut short) and three continued histories (confirmed, the content set naming another hash for leaf 4, the part of leaf 4 missing).
- One resident copy: sixteen small worlds, each wanting the large asset (1 MiB of payload) and each scripted to receive it at a tick of its own; the allocator counts the live blocks of exactly the size of that part, midway (half of the worlds hold it) and at the end, then with a deliberate second copy as a control.

The self-checks (`cargo test -p workload-asset-table`) verify the core's rules one by one on a closure of three levels (a batch wants its dependencies and answers them whatever the identifier order; an arrival no want names changes nothing; the fall happens at the fixed point after the last want ended and takes a released parent's dependencies; the gate reads the pending record, a root without its closure keeping it closed; a change of hash is an event and a marked answer is none; a command of an entity that is not alive is refused; the table and the log round trip and are refused when cut or trailing; a part is validated), that the oracle with the specification's parameters is the reference run, and every scenario on the small size over 100 ticks, the readers of the save run inside the test process.

## Environment

Research CI, workflow `research-ci.yml`, five cells (see [`spikes/README.md`](../README.md)), run on demand with `-f spike=workload-asset-table`, in the release profile and, for the identities, in the unoptimized profile (`-f profile=dev`), which measures the small size over 200 ticks and says so in its report. Rust toolchain pinned in [`rust-toolchain.toml`](../rust-toolchain.toml), default target features of each target. Standard library only, plus the reference workload crate. The cost of a handle is judged on one quiet machine outside the matrix (an AMD Ryzen 9 9950X3D, 16 cores and 32 threads, Windows 11); the hosted runners give the ordinal reading only. The loader's delays and the landing order of a revision are drawn from the clock, so no two runs see the same real timings: the hypotheses are about what stays identical anyway.

## Result

Measured on 2026-11-12 by the research CI on the five cells, in the release profile (run 43211002463) and in the unoptimized profile (run 43211017303; the small size over 200 ticks), and three times the same day on the founder's machine in the release profile (worker counts 1, 4 and 8, a run of 67 seconds). The raw measurements and the environment of each cell are in the runs' artifacts. On every cell the oracle with every leaf at the specification's parameters equals the sequential reference (run hash `0x9f50cc4763ae733c`).

- Hypothesis 1 holds on the machine it is judged on, without margin, and the hosted cells read it above the threshold. On the founder's machine, over three runs of five rounds each, the world with 1 024 distinct assets runs at 0.961 to 0.967 of the world with no asset at 1 worker and at 0.958 to 0.965 at 4 workers (ratios of the medians; 0.956 to 0.962 for the bests): a cost of 3.3 to 4.2 percent against a threshold of 5. With 16 assets the ratio is 0.978 to 0.989, with one asset 0.986 to 0.995. On the cells, the ratio of the medians at 1 024 assets is 0.961 and 0.951 (1 and 4 workers) on Linux x86_64, 0.951 and 0.941 on Linux ARM64, 0.946 and 0.943 on Windows ARM64, 0.939 and 0.932 on Windows x86_64, and 0.911 and 0.848 on the macOS runner, whose rounds disagree by more than the effect (its bests give 0.916 and 0.972): a cost of 3.9 to 6.8 percent, above 5 percent on three of the four cells whose rounds agree. The generational index form, recorded beside, runs at 0.992 to 0.997 on the founder's machine and at 0.982 to 0.995 on those four cells. The pass resolves 40 identifiers per tick with one asset (one per chunk), 640 with 16 and 10 000 with 1 024, where every entity of a chunk names an asset of its own; the handle forms allocate nothing per tick (the timed step counts 1 009 allocations with no asset, one per tick from the workload's own grid, and 1 023 with one asset). Every world of every round gives the reference hashes on every cell.
- Hypothesis 2 holds on the five cells: one state run hash at every worker count on every cell (`0xfdeb1fe31c186670` in the release profile, the founder's machine included), every tick equal to the oracle's. The two owners are the entities of slots 12 and 1, despawned by the workload at ticks 325 and 788: the asset keeps its two wants until 325, one until 788, is still available with no want after tick 788 and is gone from the table after tick 789, with no record at tick 789 in the input log (the log's records are at ticks 1, 50, 100, 110, 150, 160, 250, 900 and 905; 586 bytes). The root wanted at tick 150 is answered at tick 160 by one batch of five arrivals that leaves its group and its three leaves wanted by the core and available; released at tick 250, it is still in the table during that tick, and the five assets are gone after tick 251, with no record there either. The arrival for the asset no want names is in the log at tick 50 and leaves the table's hash as it was. The log read back from its bytes replays the run at another worker count. The table after a fifth of the run is 542 bytes, with one hash on every cell (`0x0ece087d03115844`).
- Hypothesis 3 holds on the five cells: the nine runs of each cell (three delays at three worker counts) give one state run hash (`0x584786dfbb903165`), equal to the oracle's at every tick. The loader's delays were 24 to 193 ms from the clock and 100 ms, and the gate stood closed for about that long (98.1 to 101.1 ms for the delay of 100 ms on the four Linux and Windows cells and the founder's machine, up to 171 ms on the macOS runner, whose sleeps overshoot); with no delay the answer stood in the pending record before the boundary was reached in most runs, and the gate then never closed. In every run the records of the input log are at ticks 1, 200, 201, 400, 401, 600 and 601, one per tick: the closure of three levels (the root, its group, three leaves) is one batch of five arrivals at tick 201; the corrupt root is one failed arrival at tick 401, which opens the gate, emits its event, fails the instance made from it and is named by the step with its reason; before tick 601 the step that does not wait and the step with a 30 ms deadline both answer with no tick run and the unanswered root named, the second after 30.0 to 31.5 ms; the two commands the host then posts are one record at tick 601, after which the world steps to the end.
- Hypothesis 4 holds on the five cells: the eight payloads landed one by one between 2 and 193 ms after the revision was handed over, in an order that is neither the leaves' nor the identifiers', while the world stepped 71 to 261 ticks between the first payload and the batch; the batch is one record of eight arrivals in identifier order (at ticks 201 to 371 depending on the run); the world's hashes equal the oracle switched at that tick, and differ at that tick from each of the seven worlds that would have applied the first one to seven payloads alone; the two revisions posted before one drain are one record of eight arrivals at tick 801, the leaves 0 to 3 at the later hash; the log (768 bytes) replays the run at 1, 4 and 8 workers.
- Hypothesis 5 holds on the five cells: the two worlds have equal components at every tick and different state hashes from the tick of the arrival on, and the pair of table hashes (39 bytes each, `0xc2a30f8199216488` and `0x9ef0841d3c97cb9a`) is the same on every cell, as are the table hashes of the three scenarios above.
- Hypothesis 6 holds on the five cells, each of the six readers running as a process of its own. The save at tick 500 is 250 589 bytes; during the run, the reload of leaf 4 at tick 300 instantiated its instance a second time. Read as a new run: the first step attempted before the answers runs no tick and names the twelve assets awaited; the twelve answers are the one record of tick 501, all marked. With the same hashes the state hashes equal the uninterrupted run's from tick 500 to the end, with no event on the answers and the instance still at two instantiations. With leaf 4 answered at another hash, the hashes equal the oracle that changes leaf 4 at tick 501, with no event and no third instantiation. With leaf 4's artifact cut short, the step names it with its reason, the instance is failed, and the world steps to the end on the oracle that takes leaf 4 away at tick 501. Read to continue its history: confirmed, it replays the recorded record to the same state hashes and its own log is the recorded bytes, with no confirmation in it; with the content set naming another hash for leaf 4, and with the part of leaf 4 missing, the read is refused, naming leaf 4 and the recorded hash `0xf2f2735ba757bbbd`.
- Hypothesis 7 holds on the five cells: the artifact (1 048 596 bytes) is read once; with eight of the sixteen worlds holding the asset available and eight not, then with all sixteen, the allocator counts one live block of that size, a peak of one, and two with the deliberate second copy; the store's entry is referenced by eight then sixteen worlds; the sixteen worlds give sixteen run hashes.

The unoptimized builds give the same verdicts for hypotheses 2 to 7 on the five cells, with one hash per scenario across the cells for their size; their cost ratios are not a judgment of hypothesis 1.

Not measured: more than one asset type and a kernel that reads more than two parameters through a handle; a handle resolved in more than one pass of a tick; a table of more than 1 024 entries; a want made by a system inside a tick (every want here is a command or a core want); a gate with several worlds waiting on one loader; a save whose table holds unanswered wants; peers. The gate's evaluation copies the table when a required asset is unanswered, and its cost at a large table is not measured.

The result holds for the reference workload version 1 at this toolchain, on these runner images and the founder's machine.
