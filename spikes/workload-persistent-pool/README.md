# Reference workload on a persistent worker pool

Throwaway prototype of the fundamental architecture phase (the concurrency model of the runtime), never product code. It steps the [reference workload](../reference-workload/README.md) the way the harness host of the game testing research does, one tick per call, on a worker pool owned by the runtime instance and persistent across steps, against the step-scoped pool every earlier spike used (threads spawned at every `step` call and joined before it returns, which the [runtime skeleton](../runtime-skeleton/README.md) measured at 0.28 to 0.49 milliseconds per one-tick step at 4 workers on the hosted runners). It measures, instead of assuming, whether a world stepped on the persistent pool gives the reference hashes at every worker count, alone, with two worlds alternating and with sixteen worlds stepped concurrently from sixteen threads on one pool; what a one-tick step costs on each pool; what a step after a pause and the idle pool cost; whether a shared pool serves the harness pattern as fast as sixteen step-scoped pools; whether a failed world leaves the shared pool serving the others; and whether long batches run at the same throughput on both pools.

## Layout

- `src/world.rs`: the world, a trimmed copy of the [test harness](../workload-test-harness/README.md) spike's world (the reference workload on the dependency-counting tick graph, stepped by whole ticks, the snapshot restored into a new world, the panic caught at the task boundary that fails its world), without the divergence injection, the group hashes and the extraction extension point. The graph owns no threads any more: it is a job that an executor runs, exposing claimable tasks in its own claim order and telling the executor, after each task, what to wake. The determinism of the hashes depends on the graph's dependencies alone, never on which worker runs which chunk, so the same world steps on either pool.
- `src/pool.rs`: the two pool kinds behind one small interface (`Executor::run` runs a job to completion with the calling thread as worker 0 of the step). The step-scoped team is the control: exactly what the skeleton and harness spikes do today, `workers - 1` threads spawned for the step and joined before `step` returns, each waiting with the measured policy (a spin bounded at 50 microseconds, then a park) when nothing is claimable. The persistent pool belongs to a `Runtime` for the instance's life: `workers - 1` threads that wait with the same policy when no job has claimable work, and claim tasks from any active job, the jobs in submission order and each job in the world's own claim order; several calling threads may each have a job active (the harness host steps many worlds from many test threads), a job's caller returns from `step` when the job's last task completes, and the pool is dropped with the runtime, its workers exiting then. A registered job is reached by the workers through a raw pointer that the caller publishes in a ring of slots and retires before `step` returns, guarded by one hazard pointer per worker (the sequence of the job it reads) so that the caller waits until no worker reads the job. Each persistent worker counts the time it spends spinning, its parks, the unparks it receives and the parks that returned without work, with one `Instant::now()` when it starts to wait and one when it finds work or parks.
- `src/main.rs`: the scenarios and the report, one JSON document on standard output, with the self-check that runs the whole report on the small size.

## Hypotheses

With the reference workload, version 1, default size (10 000 entities, 1 000 ticks), wander system off, compiled by the pinned Rust toolchain at default target features, at worker counts 1, 2, 4 and 8 plus the machine's available parallelism when it is not among them:

1. **Hashes.** A world stepped on the persistent pool gives the sequential reference's per-tick hashes at every worker count, in batches of 1, 10, 100 and 1 000 ticks; so do two worlds with different seeds stepped alternately on the same pool from one thread (each matching its own reference), and sixteen worlds stepped concurrently from sixteen threads sharing one pool, one tick per call (the sixteen share one seed, so every world's hashes equal the reference's). Refuted by one hash that differs.
2. **Cost per one-tick step.** The overhead of a one-tick step is the wall time of 1 000 calls of `step(1)` minus the wall time of one call of `step(1000)`, divided by 1 000, each after a 10-tick warm-up on a separate world. On the persistent pool it is under 50 microseconds at 4 workers on every machine, against 280 to 490 microseconds on the step-scoped pool on the hosted runners; both pools are reported at every worker count with the ratio step-scoped over persistent. Refuted if the persistent pool's overhead exceeds 50 microseconds at 4 workers, or exceeds a fifth of the step-scoped pool's on the same machine.
3. **Wake-up after a pause.** In a frame loop the host sleeps between steps, so the workers are parked when the next step arrives. 1 000 ticks stepped one per call with a pause of 1 millisecond and of 16 milliseconds between calls (`thread::sleep`), the step's own wall time (excluding the pause) per tick reported as mean and maximum, on both pools, at 1 and 4 workers. The persistent pool's step after a pause costs less than the step-scoped pool's step after the same pause (the unpark of parked threads is cheaper than a spawn), and its mean stays under 100 microseconds above the batch-of-1000 per-tick time at 4 workers. Refuted by a persistent mean at or above the step-scoped mean at the same workers and pause, or by an excess of 100 microseconds or more at 4 workers.
4. **Idle cost.** A persistent pool of 4 and of the machine's parallelism, idle for one second after a step, spins for at most one spin bound (50 microseconds) per worker and then parks: the total spin microseconds and the parks per worker over the idle second are reported, with whether the worker was already parked when the step returned (a step's last tasks run alone, so the other workers park before the step ends and park no more). Every worker is parked at the end of the idle second, its spin time over the second is under 200 microseconds (the bound plus the timer's granularity) and it parks at most once over the second, exactly once when it was not parked already. Refuted by a worker that spins 200 microseconds or more, parks twice, or is not parked at the end of the second.
5. **The harness pattern on a shared pool.** Sixteen worlds of the same seed, each stepped 1 000 ticks one tick per call by its own thread (the test harness spike's pattern), three ways: (a) sixteen step-scoped pools of 4 workers (today's situation: 64 threads spawned at every round of steps), (b) one persistent pool whose worker count is the machine's available parallelism, shared by the sixteen calling threads, and (c) one persistent pool of 4 workers shared by the sixteen threads, which shows what over-subscription of the pool by calling threads costs. For each: the wall time of the whole run and whether every world's hashes equal the reference. The processor time is not measured: the standard library has no portable way to read a process's processor time, and the spike adds no dependency. (b) is not slower than (a) and every hash holds in all three. Refuted if (b) is slower than (a) by more than 10 percent, or by one hash that differs.
6. **Fault isolation on a shared pool.** Two worlds stepped alternately on one persistent pool from one thread, in batches of 10; a panic injected in a task of the second world at tick 300 (in the second chunk of the repulsion pass, as the skeleton and harness spikes do) fails that world only: the first world continues to tick 1 000 with the reference's hashes, the failed world refuses further steps and reports the failure's tick, node, chunk and worker, and the pool keeps serving (the first world's remaining steps and a fresh world stepped to the end afterwards). Holds at 1, 2 and 4 workers. Refuted by a hash that differs in the first world, a failed world that steps again, a failure reported at another tick, node or chunk, or a step refused by the pool afterwards.
7. **Throughput of long batches.** A world stepped 1 000 ticks in one call reaches the same ticks per second on the persistent pool as on the step-scoped pool within 3 percent at every worker count: the pool kind costs nothing when the steps are long. Refuted by a difference above 3 percent in either direction, which would mean the pools differ in their waiting or claiming, not only in their spawn.

## Method

The binary computes the sequential reference run once per seed, then runs the scenarios in process on one world type and two executors. The hashes scenario steps one world per worker count and batch on a persistent pool of that worker count, then two worlds of different seeds alternately in batches of 10 on one pool, then sixteen worlds from sixteen threads on one pool one tick per call, and compares every world's per-tick hashes with its reference. The step cost and the long batch throughput come from the same measurement: for each worker count and each pool, a separate world is stepped 10 ticks as a warm-up, a fresh world is stepped 1 000 times by one tick and another fresh world once by 1 000 ticks, both timed by wall clock; the overhead per step is the difference divided by 1 000 and the throughput is the ticks of the one batch over its wall time. The pause scenario sleeps the stated pause before each of 1 000 one-tick steps and times each step alone. The idle scenario steps a world 10 ticks on a fresh pool, resets the workers' instruments the moment the step returns (a spin that began before the reset is charged in full when it ends), sleeps one second and reads them. The harness pattern spawns sixteen threads, each creating its world and stepping it 1 000 times by one tick, and times the whole run from before the spawn to after the last join. The fault isolation scenario alternates two worlds in batches of 10 on one pool with the panic armed in the second world's repulsion pass at tick 300 (tick 12 of a 40-tick run), and after the failure steps the first world to the end and a fresh world to the end on the same pool.

Every timed scenario runs after a warm-up. The step cost, the pause and the throughput scenarios, which are short, take the best of three alternating rounds (a round runs every worker count on the step-scoped pool then on the persistent pool, so that a disturbance hits both pools alike): the shortest wall time of each of the two timings for the costs (the overhead is derived from the two bests), the lowest mean for the pause (with the maximum of that round) and the fastest batch for the throughput. The pause scenario measures its own batch-of-1000 reference per pool and worker count the same way, so that its excess does not depend on the step cost scenario's worker counts. The hashes, idle, harness and fault scenarios run once. A build with debug assertions measures the small size, as the harness spike does, and the report says which size ran. A persistent pool is created per scenario and worker count and dropped after it (the runtime proposal has one per process; the prototype needs several worker counts in one process), and every scenario creates fresh worlds. The injected panics are kept out of the output by a panic hook that prints every other panic.

The self-checks (`cargo test -p workload-persistent-pool`) run on the small size (1 000 entities, 40 ticks): the persistent pool reproduces the sequential hashes at 1, 2, 3 and 8 workers in batches of 1, 7 and 40; two worlds on one pool from one thread each match their reference; sixteen worlds from sixteen threads on one pool all match the reference; a panic in one world fails only that world and the pool serves the other world and a fresh one afterwards, at 1, 2 and 4 workers; the step-scoped pool still matches the reference at 1, 2, 3 and 8 workers (the control is the skeleton's); the snapshot round trip is byte-identical and a truncated snapshot is refused; the workers' idle instrument reports parks, unparks and the parked state; the pool shuts down when its runtime is dropped (within a timeout, so that a leaked worker fails the test instead of hanging it) and a fresh runtime follows a dropped one; the ring of job slots is reused after two rings of jobs; and the whole report runs on the small size with every determinism, isolation and instrument check passing (the function `main` and the test share, parameterized by size and tick count; the timing thresholds are measured, not asserted, since the small size and a loaded machine do not meet them).

## Environment

Research CI, workflow `research-ci.yml`, five cells (see [`spikes/README.md`](../README.md)), run on demand with `-f spike=workload-persistent-pool`. Rust toolchain pinned in [`rust-toolchain.toml`](../rust-toolchain.toml), release profile, default target features of each target; plus a local run outside the matrix on the founder's machine, an AMD Ryzen 9 9950X3D (16 cores, 32 threads), Windows 11 Professional build 26200, native MSVC toolchain, where the available parallelism is 32 and the worker counts are therefore 1, 2, 4, 8 and 32. The hosted runners have 4 virtual CPUs (3 on macOS), which bounds what the shared pool can show there: on those cells the pool of the machine's parallelism is a pool of 4 (3 on macOS), the same as the over-subscribed pool of hypothesis 5 (c). Timings from hosted runners compare per processor only. The local run shared the machine with another spike's build and run; the Result section says which run was kept.

## Result

Local run of 2026-11-09 on the founder's machine (AMD Ryzen 9 9950X3D, 32 CPUs, Windows 11 build 26200, native MSVC toolchain, release profile, default size, 1 000 ticks, worker counts 1, 2, 4, 8 and 32), outside the matrix; not yet run on the cells. The spike ran twice while another spike built and ran on the same machine; the two runs agree within 2 percent on every timing, and the second run, made with the final code, is the one kept (its figures below, its raw measurements in the local run's output directory). The sequential reference runs at 950 ticks per second; one hash of the state (the serial task that ends every one-tick step, which a batch overlaps with the next tick's spatial hashing) takes 180 microseconds.

- Hypothesis 1 holds: the 20 batch runs (five worker counts, batches of 1, 10, 100 and 1 000), the two worlds alternating at every worker count (each matching its own reference, their final hashes different) and the sixteen worlds stepped one tick per call from sixteen threads at every worker count (80 worlds) all give the sequential reference's per-tick hashes on the persistent pool.
- Hypothesis 2 fails on this machine, for a reason the hypothesis did not account for: the persistent pool's overhead per one-tick step is 144.6 microseconds at 4 workers, above the 50-microsecond threshold and 0.64 of the step-scoped pool's 226.9 (the threshold asked for a fifth). The overhead is nearly flat across worker counts on the persistent pool (115 to 167 microseconds from 2 to 32 workers, 4.7 at 1 worker) because most of it is not the pool's: a one-tick step ends with the tick's hash run alone (180 microseconds here), while a batch overlaps that hash with the next tick's spatial hashing, so the definition of the overhead charges the lost overlap to every one-tick step at two workers or more, whatever the pool. The pool's own share is what the two pools differ by: 82 microseconds at 4 workers, 170 at 8 and 688 at 32 saved by the persistent pool against the spawn and join of the step-scoped threads, whose overhead grows with the worker count (227 at 4, 337 at 8, 821 at 32 workers) while the persistent pool's does not. The ratio step-scoped over persistent is 1.4 at 2 workers, 1.6 at 4, 2.0 at 8 and 6.2 at 32. A one-tick stepping API that wants a per-step cost under 50 microseconds has to hide the hash as well, for example by hashing on demand or deferring the hash of the last tick to the next step; that is a question for the architecture, not for the pool.
- Hypothesis 3 fails in its first clause and holds in substance: at 4 workers the persistent pool's step after a pause costs 95 to 103 microseconds less than the step-scoped pool's (495 against 591 microseconds after a 1 millisecond pause, 709 against 812 after 16 milliseconds, with lower maxima too), but at 1 worker the two pools are the calling thread alone and their means differ by noise in both directions (persistent 39 microseconds lower after 1 millisecond, 18 higher after 16), which refutes "cheaper at every worker count and pause" as worded. The excess over the batch per tick at 4 workers is 176 microseconds after 1 millisecond and 389 after 16, above the 100-microsecond threshold on both pools: the lost hash overlap (hypothesis 2) accounts for about 140 of them, and the pause itself costs more the longer it is on both pools (the step-scoped excess grows from 276 to 498 microseconds between the two pauses, the persistent from 176 to 389), a cache and frequency effect of the sleep, as the skeleton spike saw on macOS and on the founder's machine.
- Hypothesis 4 holds: on the pool of 4 and the pool of 32, every worker is parked at the end of the idle second, no worker parks more than once over it, no worker spins more than 50.2 microseconds over it, and no spurious wake occurs. Most workers (3 of 3 at 4 workers, 30 of 31 at 32) were already parked when the step returned, because the step's last tasks (the structural changes and the final hash) run alone for longer than the spin bound; the one worker still spinning at 32 workers spun 50.2 microseconds and parked once.
- Hypothesis 5 holds: the sixteen worlds stepped from sixteen threads take 1 033 milliseconds on one persistent pool of 32 workers against 2 200 on sixteen step-scoped pools of 4 workers (0.47 times the wall time; 1 136 milliseconds on one persistent pool of 4 workers shared by the sixteen threads, 0.52 times), and every world matches the reference in all three runs. The over-subscribed pool of 4 is 10 percent slower than the pool of 32, not more: the sixteen calling threads do most of the stepping themselves. The processor time is not measured (no portable standard library API).
- Hypothesis 6 holds at 1, 2 and 4 workers: the injected panic is caught at tick 300 in the second chunk of the repulsion pass (on worker 0 at every count in this run), the failed world's history stops at tick 299 and matches its reference up to there, its further step is refused, the first world continues to tick 1 000 with the reference's hashes on the same pool, and a fresh world stepped to the end on the same pool afterwards matches the reference.
- Hypothesis 7 holds: a world stepped 1 000 ticks in one call runs at the same ticks per second on both pools within 1.6 percent at every worker count (ratios 0.984 to 1.003).
- Beyond the hypotheses: the self-check suite exposed a lost wake-up in the step-scoped team as first written for this spike (the job armed before the threads published their handles, so a wake found no handle and the step hung under sixteen concurrent steps on a loaded machine); the team now has each worker publish its own handle before it looks for work, which the persistent pool does as well, and the suite passed every run since. The step-scoped pool's spawn cost at 32 workers (821 microseconds per step, four times the batch per tick) shows what a harness host pays today when its worlds use the machine's parallelism.

The raw measurements and the environment of the run are in the local run's output directory; the cells' results are to be added when the research CI run exists. The result holds for the reference workload version 1 at this toolchain and on this machine.
